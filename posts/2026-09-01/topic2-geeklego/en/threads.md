---

We've written design system documentation for two decades. For humans.

GeekyAnts open-sourced GeekLego last week — an AI-native design system built on Tailwind CSS v4 under MIT license — and what's new isn't the component library or the tokens. It's the machine-readable governance rules baked directly into the system.

GeekLego can tell Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, and any other agent-capable tool exactly how to use each component, how to apply design tokens, what accessibility requirements apply, and how to stay synchronized with your Figma workflows. Not after the AI generates code. Before. At generation time.

That's the governance model shift everyone's been circling for months without naming clearly: standards introduced at generation time, not enforced after.

Here's what this actually changes: design system documentation now has two simultaneous audiences — developers and agents. And the rules that matter most to agents are the same rules DesignOps has always cared about: component intent, token semantics, constraint rationale. We just didn't know we were writing for two audiences at once.

The honest question GeekLego raises: who authors the machine-readable rules? Not the developer. Not the AI. The person who understands why each constraint exists. That's not a technical role. That's a design leadership role.

Being MIT-licensed and open-source means the floor just moved. Every team that adopts GeekLego gets the same governance baseline. The senior designer's edge is no longer having a design system at all — it's having one where the intent is documented clearly enough for an agent to apply it correctly.

Design system ROI argument just changed. Not just "consistent UI" and "faster handoff." Now also: "your AI coding agent codes by your rules, not its guesses."

The quality ceiling is still yours. GeekLego just made the floor explicit.
