# Threads Post — Figma Custom Skills: The Design Documentation Finally Has a Reader
**Platform:** Threads | **Date:** 2026-07-26 | **Language:** English

---

I've been writing design documentation for a decade. Notion pages that nobody visits. Figma comments that nobody reads. Confluence wikis that are outdated the moment they're published.

We write documentation for humans. And humans ignore it.

Figma Custom Skills flipped the audience.

---

A Skill is a Markdown file. You write your team's conventions — which libraries to use, which naming rules to follow, how to structure components, when to check accessibility — and the agent reads it before every single task.

Not when it feels relevant. Not when you remind it. Every time.

The reader finally shows up.

---

The implication is more uncomfortable than it sounds.

If you can't write your design conventions clearly enough for an agent to follow them, there are two explanations: either your conventions aren't actually systematic, or you haven't understood them well enough to articulate them.

Both are design ops problems. Both are worth fixing.

---

The Figma blog put the framing cleanly: "Design systems give the agent the right components. Skills layer on the rest of your team's expertise."

Skills can encode brand voice guidelines, accessibility standards, component variant rules, review checklists — the things that live in senior designers' heads and die when they leave.

That institutional knowledge portability is the underrated part.

---

I've been calling this "constraint architecture" for a while. The idea that the real design work is defining the boundaries within which AI generates, not doing the generation itself. Skills are the most explicit instantiation of that I've seen.

You write the constraints. You version them. You share them across your team. The agent applies them without drift.

---

Honest caveat: skill quality is a new discipline. A vague Skill produces vague output. Generic instructions produce generic results. The precision of the writing is where the work actually lives.

Most designers haven't been trained to write machine-executable instructions. That gap is real.

---

Real headline: this isn't a Figma feature update.

It's a new job description for senior designers. You're not just designing systems. You're writing curriculum for agents that will execute your judgment at scale.

The question worth sitting with: what does your team's agent curriculum currently look like?
